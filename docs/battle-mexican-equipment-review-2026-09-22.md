## Evidence and design

The National Park Service’s archaeological report [A Thunder of Cannon, chapter 4](https://npshistory.com/series/archeology/scrc/52/chap4.htm) supports a tall cylindrical infantry shako, tailcoat, cartridge equipment and the India Pattern Brown Bess. It describes different regimental facings, militia clothing, seasonal trousers and shortages; regulation dress did not uniformly reach soldiers. The model therefore uses a blue coat with red collar/cuffs, light canvas trousers and plain crossbelts as a **clothing-family analogy**, without identifying a regiment. Its unnumbered plate, simple pompon and plain buttons avoid invented insignia. The firearm has an approximately 39-inch barrel, pinned wooden stock, ramrod pipes, exposed flint action and an offset triangular socket bayonet of approximately 15 inches. Figure 16 was visually inspected for the weapon outline.

[Appendix C](https://npshistory.com/series/archeology/scrc/52/appc.htm) distinguishes the earlier bell-crowned shako from the cylindrical form after 1839 and describes mixed clothing issues. That chronology supports the reviewed war window; it does not authorize automatic use across all Mexican forces after 1839.

The NPS [Lock, Stock, and Barrel](https://www.nps.gov/paal/learn/historyculture/lock-stock-barrel.htm) identifies the Brown Bess in Mexican service. Its close-up was inspected: the lower weapon supplies a visual reference for the flint mechanism, whereas the upper weapon is an American Harper’s Ferry 1816. The modern pan guards in this photograph are omitted. This is **not** treated as a provenanced photograph of a Mexican 1846 specimen.

The Secretaría de Marina’s [history, chapter 3, printed page 151](https://www.semar.gob.mx/unhicun/publicaciones_historicas/serie_unhicun/historia_infanteria/historia_infanteria5.pdf) reproduces the clothing simplification ordered on 7 January 1848. PDF page 14 was rendered and inspected. That change is a conservative reason to stop eligibility at 1847; it neither proves immediate universal compliance in 1848 nor retroactively establishes the exact model combination in 1846.

A primary 1847 commercial print, [James Cameron, Battle of Cerro Gordo, Amon Carter Museum 1972.40](https://www.cartermuseum.org/collection/battle-cerro-gordo-april-18th-1847-197240), was also inspected. Its figures are schematic and its applied colors cannot establish one exact Mexican uniform. It is not used to turn a painted detail into universal equipment evidence. The NPS modern wayside painting is likewise not mislabeled as contemporary art. A Smithsonian generic militia cap with a war association and contradictory Port Isabel uniform metadata were not used to establish Mexican infantry identity.

These event sources establish affiliation and an infantry component. They do **not** establish that all troops wore the illustrated clothes or carried identical weapons. The army totals retain cavalry, artillery, militia and other personnel. No infantry share is inferred. In particular, the light trousers do not establish universal summer dress at the cold February battle of Buena Vista. No eligibility is added for independence-era Mexico, 1836, 1848, later wars, or unknown coalitions. Cerro Gordo is not included in this proposal.
